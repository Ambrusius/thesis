\chapter{CERN}

The European Organization for Nuclear Research, CERN, was initially a planned by 12 founding nations in 1953. It has since had its ranks bolstered by several new member nations, bringing the total up to 24.

The goal of the organization is to provide a place where scientists from all over the world can come together to conduct research in the field of particle physics. CERN is most famous for the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), a particle accelerator that is the largest and most powerful in the world. The LHC is located in a 27 km long tunnel beneath the Franco-Swiss border near Geneva, Switzerland.

The LHC has four main detectors; ATLAS, CMS, LHCb and ALICE. The ATLAS and CMS detectors are general purpose detectors, while LHCb is designed to study B-mesons and ALICE is designed to study heavy-ion collisions.

In 2012 the ATLAS and CMS collaborations jointly announced the discovery of the Higgs Boson, a particle that had been theorized to exist since the 1960s. The discovery was made by analyzing data from the LHC.

\section{ATLAS}
A Toroidal LHC ApparatuS (ATLAS) is, as mentioned, a general purpose detector at the LHC.
It is located 100m below the ground, formed around the LHC beam-pipe. It is 44 meters long and 25 meters in diameter and weighs 7000 tons.

ATLAS contains layers of detectors, wrapped around the beam-pipe. These detectors are used to measure the properties of particles that are produced in the collisions in the LHC. The innermost layer is the pixel detector, followed by the semiconductor tracker (SCT) and the transition radiation tracker (TRT). These detectors are used to measure the trajectories of charged particles. The electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL) and the hadronic calorimeter (HCAL) are used to measure the energy of particles. The muon spectrometer is used to measure the trajectories of muons. All this is built in a large magnet system, consisting of a central solenoid and two end-cap toroids. The magnet system is used to bend the paths of charged particles, using the shape of the curve for the measurement of their momentum. [https://atlas.cern/Discover/Detector]




\chapter{Boosted Decision Trees}

The selection that this thesis is attempting to beat is a simple single dimensional cuts-based selection. This selection looks at one parameter at a time and makes a decision to either discard or keep the event.

We can consider a simple improvement of this single dimensional selection by using a multidimensional selection. Where we might look at several parameters where some might cause the event to be discarded, but others show the event is a very high probability of being signal.

This is essentially a decision tree. A decision tree is an algorithm that makes a decision based on a series of questions. The key difference is that the choice is not discard or keep, but rather a choice of which question to ask next. The final decision is then made based on the answers to these questions.

A decision tree is made up of leaves and nodes. A node is a question and a leaf is a decision. The tree starts with a single node, this is the root node. The root node asks a question and based on the answer the tree will move to one of the child-nodes. This process is repeated until a final decision is made.

\section{Evaluation of model}
Comparing the model with the existing, single dimensional cuts. Is simple enough for MC, but for data where we have no labels the process is more involved.

To get an estimate for the quality of the selection we look at the invariant mass of the selected events, for the Z-decay we expect a peak at the Z-mass. Firstly, this peak should be higher allowing for more statistics. Secondly, we would prefer a better signal to background ratio.

To get an estimate for the signal to background ratio we fit a function to the invariant mass that both contains a signal shape and a background shape. These shapes are then normalized to the number of both types of events.
The function we fit is a Crystal Ball function (CB) for the signal convoluted with a Breit-Wigner distribution.
For the background fit we use an exponential distribution.
The CB function is a Gaussian function with a power-law tail on one side. % The function is defined as:

\section{The model}

In this section I will describe the model, the data pre-processing and the training of the model.

The goal of the model is to boil a large parameter spae down to a single number; the probability that this lepton pair comes from a Z-boson.
A single number has a lot of benefits over the existing, cuts-based method. First and foremost it is a lot easier to loosen the selection,
this is useful if one wants more statistics. especially in the case of the H\(\to Z\gamma\) decay where we know that the background from 
Z\(\to\ell\ell\) in the final Higgs signal is minimal. [SOURCE SOURCE SOURCE].





\subsection{overall structure}

It is important that the model does not train on the invariant mass of the leptons. This is because for Z-selection, we expect a peak at the Z-mass.
So should the model simply learn this mass, then it would be useless for the selection.

To combat this we are very selective with what variables we feed into the model and when we use them.

We essentially train two models, (three for the case of \(Z\to\mu\mu\)).
One model is trained on the isolation of the leptons, the other is trained on the identification of the leptons.
Then we feed the values from these two models into a third model, along with information on how close the origins of the lepton tracks are from each other.


PID - Particle Identification
For electons we do not need to train a model for the PID, as there already exists a model for electron identification that performs very well.
\begin{verbatim}
    http://cds.cern.ch/record/2809283/files/ATL-PHYS-PUB-2022-022.pdf
\end{verbatim}

This model will not only provide an electron-score, but also other scores that attempts to give information on what type of electron it is.

meaning that the meta-model will not only be provided with the identification score that the leptons are in fact electrons. 
But might also know that the lepton comes from a number of background events that are outside of the area we are interrested in.



\subsection{Data pre-processing}

First we need to pre-process the data. Data and MC from the ATLAS detector comes in the form of a ROOT file. The cuts-based selection is then performed directly 
on these files, returning an Ntuple with the specific events that might be needed for future data processing and only containing the variables that are needed.

Our model will need additional variables and it will need more events, if nothing else for background for the training.
So first step is to adapt the C++ code that does the cuts-based selection to output an Ntuple with the variables that we need.

The adaptation that we made does the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Load the ROOT file.
    \item Apply the cuts-based selection.
    \item for events that pass the selection, save the events with aditional variables.
    \item for events that do not pass the selection, run the algorithm again, this time ignoring the single cut that failed. Then save the event based on a condition that we define.
\end{enumerate}

The adaptation will not only provide the benefit of having more data, but also having the information of what cut failed for the event. 
This means that even without directly implementing the model, we might be able to provide feedback on where the cuts-based selection might be too strict.


For signal MC we use \(Z\to\ell\ell\) and for background we use \(t\bar{t}\). We feed this through the adapted code and get two Ntuples.


\subsection{background}

For isolation bagkground is simple. we use the \(Z\to\ell\ell\) and \(t\bar{t}\) MC Ntuples that all contain pairs of leptons.
We then unpair this data to look at individual leptons that can be tagged as "Isolated" or not. the isolated leptons are considered signal and everything else is background.

for the Z-model in the \(Z\to e e\) case we use the same samples. but this time to specify signal we first need to know that the pair is from a Z-boson.
This is needed because with the loosed selection we only see about \(70\%\) true-Z pairst from the \(Z\to e e\) sample.
background is everything from the \(t\bar{t}\) sample.
